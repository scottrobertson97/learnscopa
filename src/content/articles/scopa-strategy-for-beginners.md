## Make legal choices before clever choices

Your first strategic advantage is knowing what you are allowed to do. A tempting sum is irrelevant when your played card has a single match. A possible capture cannot be declined after you choose that card. Getting these rules right reduces the number of decisions you need to make.

Before playing, compare all three hand cards with the table. One might match a single card, another might collect a sum, and a third might capture nothing. Your freedom lies in choosing which hand card to play and, when appropriate, which legal capture to select. Our [capture lessons](/learn) are a good way to make that scan automatic.

## 1. Look at the table you will leave

Beginners often look only at the cards they can collect. Also picture the cards left behind. If you take a five and leave a lone six, the opponent can sweep with a six. If you take a different legal group and leave a larger total, you may deny that simple reply.

Any table whose combined capturing value is greater than 10 cannot be swept in one ordinary move, because no played card is worth more than 10. That is a useful observation, not a guarantee of safety on future turns. The next capture can reduce the layout again.

## 2. Treat sweep risks as information

Leaving a single card or a low total can offer a sweep, but your opponent must actually hold the right rank. You do not know their whole hand. Avoiding every conceivable sweep may mean passing up a better capture with another card.

Compare the visible tradeoffs. Are you securing the settebello? Taking several coins? Giving up a likely point to avoid an uncertain one? Scopa rewards judgment under incomplete information. A good decision can still meet an unlucky reply.

## 3. Notice coins and sevens

Coins matter because most coins is a separate scoring category. The seven of coins also has its own point. All sevens are valuable for primiera, and sixes are next best. A capture of two low cards is not automatically better than a capture of one valuable seven.

For example, if your played seven can take either a single seven of cups or a single seven of coins, both choices are legal. The coins seven guarantees the settebello point when captured. If neither choice involves the settebello, look at the suits already represented in your pile and what each choice leaves on the table.

## 4. Build a collection across suits

Primiera needs at least one card from every suit. Do not mentally reduce it to “who has more sevens?” A strong seven is useful, but a player missing an entire suit does not qualify at all.

You do not need to continually rearrange a face-down capture pile. As a beginner, practice remembering which suits you have collected. During a teaching game, agree in advance to discuss the piles openly. That is a learning aid, rather than a claim about what every competitive table allows.

## 5. Remember ranks a little at a time

There are four cards of each capturing value. If you have seen all four fives enter capture piles, no five remains to appear in a later hand. Remembering one relevant rank is more manageable than trying to memorize the whole deck immediately.

Start with the sevens, or the rank that threatens a sweep on the current table. Do not count a face-up table card as gone: it is still available to capture. Separate “seen” from “already captured” in your thinking.

## 6. Pay attention near the end

The last capturer receives the leftovers after the final play. Late captures can therefore decide most cards or most coins. Notice who currently holds that role and whether your remaining hand can change it.

Remember that a sweep on the final play does not earn a point. Earlier sweeps do. With the stock exhausted, think about collection points and leftovers as well as clearing the table. Then [count the full deal](/scoring), talk through one interesting choice, and try another. A small habit improved each game is plenty of progress.
