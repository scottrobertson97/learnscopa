## Begin with a table, not a score

The quickest way to make Scopa feel complicated is to explain every scoring detail before anyone touches a card. For a first practice, focus on one thing: playing a card and seeing what it captures. The score will have more meaning once you have a pile to count.

Scopa is a game of collecting cards from a shared table. Both players start with three cards in hand, and four cards lie face up in the middle. On your turn, you play exactly one card. You either take cards with it or leave it on the table for a later turn. You can learn the basic rhythm without memorizing a single Italian word, although the words quickly become part of the pleasure.

## Read the deck before you deal

A traditional Latin-suited deck contains coins, cups, swords, and clubs. Each suit has Ace through 7 and three court cards. Ace is 1; the numbered cards keep their values. Fante, the foot soldier, is 8. Cavallo, the rider, is 9. Re, the king, is 10.

Fan out the three court cards before starting. On a real deck they may have no corner numbers, so the person standing, the person riding, and the crowned person become your clues. Our [interactive first lesson](/learn) labels those values while you learn them. If you have a familiar 52-card deck instead, [adapt it to forty cards](/articles/playing-scopa-with-a-regular-deck).

## Make the opening deal

Choose a dealer, shuffle, and let the opponent cut. Deal three cards to each player, alternating one card at a time and starting with the non-dealer. Then turn four cards face up in the middle. The remaining stock waits face down. If three or four kings appear among those four table cards, gather everything and redeal.

The non-dealer goes first. After that, take turns. Keep your capture piles away from the table so nobody confuses cards already won with cards still available.

## Ask the same three questions each turn

First: does your played card match a single table card’s value? A five takes a five, regardless of suit. If two fives are available, choose one of them.

Second: if there is no single match, can table cards add up to your played card? A seven can take four plus three. It might also take one plus two plus four. When several combinations work, choose one group.

Third: if neither works, where does your card go? Onto the table, face up. It may help the next player, or it may come back to you later.

A capture is compulsory for the card you play. And a single match always beats a sum: with five, three, and two on the table, a played five must take the five. It cannot take three plus two instead.

## Keep going until the deck is gone

When both hands are empty, deal three more cards each. Do not refresh the table. Continue with the non-dealer first until there are no stock or hand cards left. The player who made the last capture collects any remaining table cards.

If a capture empties the table during play, call “Scopa!” and record a sweep. The final play of the entire deal is the exception: it cannot earn a sweep point. Collecting leftovers is not a sweep either.

## Count together, then play again

For the first deal, spread the piles out and use our [scoring guide](/scoring). Most cards, most coins, the seven of coins, and the best primiera can each earn one point; every valid sweep adds one. Ties for most cards, most coins, or primiera award neither player that point.

Keep a running score across deals. At the end of a deal, the higher total wins once someone reaches at least 11; tied leaders continue. Swap dealers and try another. There is no need to play quickly. Saying “I’m taking this five because it matches” is often the best first strategy.
