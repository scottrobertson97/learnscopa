## A special name for a useful card

The settebello is the seven of coins: a single card with its own scoring category in Scopa. Capture it, keep it in your pile through the end of the deal, and you earn one point. You do not need the most coins to receive that point. You do not need to win any other category either.

It is a useful card to recognize early because its value is visible and concrete. Primiera asks you to compare four suits; most cards asks you to count a whole pile. The settebello asks one simple question: who took the seven of coins?

## One card can help four ways

The settebello does more than earn its named point. Like any captured card, it counts toward the size of your pile. Like any coin, it counts toward most coins. Like any seven, it contributes 21 toward a qualified primiera when it is your best card in that suit.

These benefits are counted independently. Imagine finishing with 21 cards, six coins, the settebello, and a winning primiera. You could earn all four collection points. That is not four points for the settebello itself; it is one special point plus separate wins in the other categories. This distinction helps explain why a single card can influence a deal without having a mysterious hidden rule.

## It is not a trump card

During play, the seven of coins has capturing value 7. It follows precisely the same capture rules as a seven of cups, swords, or clubs. It cannot beat a king, collect arbitrary cards, or ignore a single matching seven on the table.

Suppose you hold it and the table contains a seven, four, and three. You must take the single seven. Although four plus three makes seven, exact-match precedence still applies. If the table instead contains only four and three, the settebello can capture both and score a sweep on an eligible turn.

Once captured, the settebello stays in that player’s pile. Players do not capture from each other’s won cards. The interesting decision happens before it has been collected.

## Notice when it becomes available

If the settebello is face up on the table and you hold a seven, you can take it. With several sevens on the table, you may choose the coins seven as your single match. That is often attractive because its named point is certain, although every move also changes the table for your opponent.

If you are holding the settebello and no capture exists, playing it will leave it exposed. Another seven can then take it. You may prefer to play a different hand card, but that is a strategic choice, not a rule requiring you to save it. If you do play it into an available capture, that capture is mandatory.

## Finding it in different decks

In a Latin-suited pack, look for seven coins rather than relying on a particular border or corner numeral. Regional patterns and manufacturers arrange their pictures differently. Our site’s illustration uses golden circular pips and is original teaching artwork, not a promise that every physical seven will look the same.

When using an adapted French-suited deck, diamonds represent coins. The seven of diamonds therefore becomes the settebello. The point is unchanged; only the suit picture differs. See our [deck guide](/cards) if you are choosing between patterns.

## Keep its importance in perspective

There is only one settebello point in a deal. A player can capture it and still lose the deal or the match. Do not abandon every useful capture just to chase it. Learn to recognize it, take sensible opportunities, and count the other categories too. Our [scoring reference](/scoring) shows how the entire result fits together.
