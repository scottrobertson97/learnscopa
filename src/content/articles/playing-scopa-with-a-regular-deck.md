## You can start with the cards you have

A regional Italian deck brings a wonderful visual character to Scopa, but it is not required to learn the game. A standard French-suited pack can provide the same forty ranks. The important work is removing the right cards and remembering that the remaining court cards have values above seven.

Use a normal 52-card pack with hearts, diamonds, clubs, and spades. Put aside any jokers before counting. Then follow these steps. You will not need to write on the cards, buy an accessory, or change the rules of capture.

## Remove the numbered 8s, 9s, and 10s

Take out all four eights, all four nines, and all four tens. That removes twelve cards and leaves forty. Keep every Ace, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, Jack, Queen, and King in every suit.

This can feel counterintuitive: Scopa uses capturing values 8, 9, and 10, so why remove those numbers? Because those values belong to the three court ranks. Keeping the printed eight, nine, and ten as well would create thirteen ranks per suit and a different deck.

Count ten cards in each suit after removing the extras. A quick second check now prevents confusion during the deal.

## Use this rank reference

| Regular card | Scopa capturing value | Latin-suited counterpart |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ace | 1 | Asso |
| 2–7 | Printed number | Same number |
| Jack | 8 | Fante |
| Queen | 9 | Cavallo |
| King | 10 | Re |

The Queen stands in for the rider because it occupies the middle court position. You are matching ranks and values, not suggesting the two illustrations depict the same character. Keep this table visible during the first few hands if it helps.

## Let diamonds stand for coins

Use diamonds as coins, hearts as cups, spades as swords, and clubs as clubs. For captures, those labels do not affect legality: a heart five can take a club five. The mapping becomes important when you score.

Most diamonds earns the most-coins point. The seven of diamonds becomes the settebello and earns its own point. Primiera needs one card from each of your four French suits and uses the usual special values. Sevens contribute 21, sixes 18, Aces 16, fives 15, fours 14, threes 13, twos 12, and Jacks, Queens, and Kings 10 each.

## Deal and capture exactly as usual

For two players, deal three cards each, then four face-up table cards. Leave the rest as stock. The non-dealer begins. If the initial table contains three or four Kings, gather the cards and redeal.

A Jack captures with value 8. If there is a Jack on the table, another played Jack must take one matching Jack. Only without a matching Jack can it take a sum such as five plus three. A Queen might take six plus three, while a King might take seven plus three. A single match still takes precedence over those combinations.

When both hands are empty, deal three more cards each without refreshing the table. Finish the stock, play the remaining hands, and award table leftovers to the last capturer. Sweeps follow the ordinary rule, including no sweep point on the final play of the entire deal.

## Make the first deal comfortable

The unfamiliar part is usually remembering Jack = 8 and Queen = 9. Say the value aloud when practicing: “I’m playing a nine, so my Queen takes the six and the three.” After a few turns, the substitution becomes easier.
